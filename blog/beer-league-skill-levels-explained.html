Beer League Skill Levels Explained: What A, B, C, and D Divisions Mean

August 3, 2026 • 6 min read • By Utah Glizzies HC

You go to sign up for a beer league and hit a wall of letters: A, B, C, D, sometimes E. Nobody tells you what they mean, and the league's own descriptions read like they were written for people who already know. Here's what the letters actually mean, why they're not consistent between rinks, and how to guess your level without embarrassing yourself in either direction.

Why Beer League Divisions Exist in the First Place

Skill divisions exist for one reason: nobody has fun in a game that's wildly mismatched. A rink that dumps former college players and total beginners onto the same ice ends up with blowout scores, frustrated new players, and bored ringers. Letter divisions are the sorting mechanism that keeps games close and keeps people coming back.

A Division: The Ringers

A-level beer league is the top of the rec pyramid. Players here are often former pro, semi-pro, or high-level collegiate skaters — people who could probably still play juniors if their knees agreed. If you haven't played organized hockey before, A division is not your on-ramp.

B Division: The Former High School and College Crowd

B-level players usually suited up in high school or college and never really stopped. They know the game inside and out — reading plays, executing systems, handling the puck under pressure — even if their conditioning isn't what it used to be.

C Division: Solid, Experienced Rec Players

C is the broad middle of adult hockey. If you've been playing casually for years, you're probably here. C-level players have the fundamentals down — multiple shooting styles, skating backward, stopping from a sprint — without the polish of a former competitive player.

D Division: True Beginners, No Experience Required

D is the entry point, built specifically for people with little or no hockey background. Expect a slower pace, patient teammates, and an emphasis on learning rather than winning. If you're coming straight from the stands after watching the Mammoth, this is where you start — we walk through the exact path in how to start playing hockey as an adult in Utah.

Why the Letters Aren't Standardized (and Why That Matters)

Here's the part that trips people up: there's no universal rulebook for what "B" or "C" means. A "C" player in one city's beer league could easily be a "B" in another market with a smaller talent pool. Utah's still a young hockey market, so divisions here can skew differently than in places like Minnesota or Ontario where the talent base runs deeper.

The letter on the schedule is a local guess, not a national standard. Always read the league's actual division description instead of assuming your last city's "C" travels with you.

How to Figure Out Your Level Before You Sign Up

  1. Read the league's own division descriptions instead of assuming letters translate city to city.
  2. Be honest about your last competitive season. "I played travel hockey until I was 14" and "I played travel hockey until college" are different divisions.
  3. When in doubt, go one level down. It's far more fun to be a strong player in a lower division than a liability in a higher one.
  4. Ask the league directly. Most Utah programs — including drop-in and learn-to-play sessions at the Mammoth Ice Center — are happy to place new players after a quick conversation or a single evaluation skate.

The Bottom Line

A, B, C, and D are useful shorthand, but they're a local guess, not a certification. Read the actual division descriptions, be honest about your background, and default to the lower level when unsure. Get the level right and you'll spend your first season having fun instead of either getting run over or bored out of your mind.
